MVE221IA-P1Two-Stage Miller Op-Amp: Spec to Post-Layout in sky130 Track AIntermediateBuild first 5-7 wk22/30

Identification

Analog and Mixed-signal IC Design (MVE221IA), Semester 2. Level: Foundational. Primary track A; secondary: C. Estimated effort: 5-7 weeks (about 60-84 focused hours at roughly 12 h/week). Carried over from the 2024 card MVE321IA-P1.

Syllabus mapping: Unit I two-stage op-amps and PSRR; Unit II stability, pole splitting and Miller compensation; lab experiments 6-7

Engineering problem

Hand-calculated sizing rarely survives simulation, and layout parasitics erode phase margin. The project closes the loop from specification to post-layout.

Proposed solution and architecture

Size a two-stage Miller-compensated op-amp from specs (gain, GBW, PM, power), simulate DC/AC/transient/corners in ngspice, draw layout in Magic or KLayout with matching care, extract parasitics, and compare pre- and post-layout.

Spec sheet→Hand sizing (gm/Id)→ngspice testbenches→Layout→PEX→Corner report

Implementation plan

  1. Study: Razavi two-stage design, Miller compensation. Gate: derive GBW and PM relations.
  2. Size: by gm/Id; Gate: DC operating point as designed.
  3. Simulate: AC loop gain, PM, slew, noise, corners. Gate: spec table.
  4. Layout and extract: Gate: DRC/LVS clean.
  5. Compare pre/post-layout; document margins.

Tools and resources

xschem, ngspice, Magic/KLayout, sky130 PDK (all free). Optionally Cadence/Synopsys (not required).

Measurable evaluation

  • Baseline: Your hand-calculation prediction.
  • Measure: DC gain, GBW, phase margin, slew rate, power, input noise, offset (MC).
  • Success looks like: Meets stated specs at TT and across corners; post-layout degradation quantified.

Report each number as measured, simulated, analytical estimate or design target. Nothing here is a pre-measured result.

Final deliverables

Schematics, layout, testbenches, report.

Career value

Competencies: Analog design flow, compensation, layout parasitics.

Resume bullet (use only after you build it and fill in measured numbers): Designed a two-stage op-amp in sky130 and quantified post-layout vs pre-layout phase margin.

Likely interview questions:

  • Why Miller compensation?
  • What is the RHP zero?
  • How does layout affect PM?
  • How did you choose gm/Id?

Risks and fallback

Corner failures. Fallback: relax specs and document.

Extensions

  • Folded-cascode or telescopic comparison
  • Use it in the biquads of MVE314TA-P3 and the SC integrator of MVE221IA-P2

MVE221IA-P2Switched-Capacitor Integrator and Bottom-Plate Sample-and-Hold: Charge Injection and kT/C in sky130 Track AIntermediateBuild next 5-7 wk23/30

Identification

Analog and Mixed-signal IC Design (MVE221IA), Semester 2. Level: Industry-oriented. Primary track A; secondary: C. Estimated effort: 5-7 weeks (about 60-84 focused hours at roughly 12 h/week). New for the 2026 scheme.

Syllabus mapping: Unit III: MOSFETs as sampling switches, switch distortion, channel charge injection, clock feedthrough, bottom-plate sampling, parasitic-insensitive SC integrator, sample-and-hold characteristics

Engineering problem

Switched-capacitor circuits are the front end of almost every ADC, but their errors (charge injection, clock feedthrough, kT/C noise, signal-dependent switch resistance) are easy to mis-estimate. This project measures each error in simulation and checks it against theory.

Proposed solution and architecture

Design an NMOS switch, a CMOS transmission gate, a bootstrapped switch and a bottom-plate sample-and-hold in sky130. Measure the charge-injection pedestal, distortion (THD and SFDR from a coherent FFT) and noise against analytical models. Then build a parasitic-insensitive SC integrator around a simple OTA and measure gain error and settling vs op-amp gain and bandwidth.

Switch variants→Bottom-plate S/H→Non-overlapping clock generator→Transient + FFT scripts→Noise analysis→SC integrator with OTA

Implementation plan

  1. Study: switched-capacitor circuits (Razavi) and sampling (Baker). Build: NMOS and TG switches with a 1 pF capacitor. Gate: on-resistance vs input voltage matches hand analysis.
  2. Measure charge injection and clock feedthrough vs switch width and input level; compare with the 50/50 charge-split estimate. Artifact: pedestal error plots. Gate: discrepancies explained.
  3. Add bottom-plate sampling and a bootstrapped switch; measure SFDR with coherent sampling and an FFT. Gate: coherent set-up verified (no spectral leakage).
  4. Measure sampled noise with transient noise or many-run statistics and compare with kT/C. Gate: within the stated statistical error.
  5. Build the parasitic-insensitive SC integrator; measure gain error and settling vs OTA gain and GBW. Write design rules for switch size and capacitor value.

Tools and resources

ngspice (transient noise supported), xschem, sky130 PDK, Python for FFT and statistics. CPU only.

Measurable evaluation

  • Baseline: A single NMOS switch with top-plate sampling.
  • Measure: Pedestal error (mV), SFDR and THD (dB), integrated noise vs kT/C, integrator gain error, settling time.
  • Success looks like: Each error source measured and matched to its model within a stated tolerance, with design rules derived. Simulation only.

Report each number as measured, simulated, analytical estimate or design target. Nothing here is a pre-measured result.

Final deliverables

Schematics or netlists, scripts, plots, 4-page report.

Career value

Competencies: Sampling circuits, SC design, coherent FFT testing, noise analysis.

Resume bullet (use only after you build it and fill in measured numbers): Characterised charge injection, distortion and kT/C noise of NMOS, TG and bootstrapped sampling switches in sky130, improving SFDR from X to Y dB (report your simulated numbers).

Likely interview questions:

  • Why does bottom-plate sampling reduce signal-dependent charge injection?
  • How does a bootstrapped switch keep its on-resistance constant?
  • Why is kT/C noise independent of switch resistance?
  • What is coherent sampling and why does an FFT test need it?

Risks and fallback

Transient noise simulation is slow; use a few long runs. Fallback: analytical noise plus a reduced Monte-Carlo.

Extensions

  • Make it fully differential with common-mode feedback
  • Use it as the front end of MVE221IA-P3

MVE221IA-P3Transistor-Level 8-bit SAR ADC in sky130 with FFT-Based ENOB and Monte-Carlo Linearity Track AResearch-gradeStretch 8-10 wk25/30

Identification

Analog and Mixed-signal IC Design (MVE221IA), Semester 2. Level: Advanced / research. Primary track A; secondary: C. Estimated effort: 8-10 weeks (about 96-120 focused hours at roughly 12 h/week). New for the 2026 scheme.

Syllabus mapping: Units III-IV: sample-and-hold, data-converter specifications, charge-scaling DAC, successive-approximation ADC; lab experiment 10 (SAR ADC design and verification)

Engineering problem

SAR ADCs are the standard choice for sensor and low-power accelerator readout, and the lab asks for one. Moving from a behavioural model to transistor level exposes comparator offset, capacitor mismatch and DAC settling, which decide the real ENOB.

Proposed solution and architecture

Design an 8-bit charge-redistribution SAR ADC in sky130: a binary-weighted capacitor DAC (optionally split), a latch comparator with a preamp, a bootstrapped sampling switch and SAR logic synthesised from Verilog. Simulate at 1 MS/s, compute ENOB, SFDR, INL and DNL from coherent FFT and ramp tests, and run Monte-Carlo on capacitor mismatch and comparator offset.

Bootstrapped S/H→Capacitor DAC→Preamp + latch comparator→SAR logic→Mixed-signal testbench→FFT + ramp analysis→Monte-Carlo

Implementation plan

  1. Study: SAR operation, charge-redistribution DACs, comparator offset (Baker; Razavi). Build: a behavioural Python model with mismatch. Gate: model ENOB vs mismatch curve.
  2. Build: capacitor DAC and switches; verify settling to half an LSB within the bit-cycle time. Gate: worst-case settling margin reported.
  3. Build: comparator; measure offset, delay vs input and kickback. Gate: offset sigma within the error budget from step 1.
  4. Integrate with SAR logic (Verilog synthesised to standard cells, or XSPICE digital models). Run coherent transient and ramp tests. Artifact: ENOB, SFDR, INL/DNL. Gate: nominal-corner ENOB documented.
  5. Run Monte-Carlo (20-50 runs) and corners; compare with the behavioural model and explain the gap.

Tools and resources

ngspice with XSPICE or synthesised cells, xschem, sky130 PDK, Yosys for the SAR logic, Python. CPU only; runs take hours, so batch them overnight.

Measurable evaluation

  • Baseline: Ideal behavioural SAR model (and the lab reference schematic if available).
  • Measure: ENOB, SNDR, SFDR, INL/DNL (LSB), power per conversion, Walden figure of merit.
  • Success looks like: A working 8-bit SAR ADC in simulation with ENOB and linearity reported at corners and under mismatch. Not silicon; layout parasitics are excluded unless extracted.

Report each number as measured, simulated, analytical estimate or design target. Nothing here is a pre-measured result.

Final deliverables

Schematics, netlists, SAR logic Verilog, scripts, data, 6-page report.

Career value

Competencies: Mixed-signal design, comparator and DAC design, data-converter characterisation.

Resume bullet (use only after you build it and fill in measured numbers): Designed an 8-bit SAR ADC in sky130 reaching X ENOB at 1 MS/s in simulation, with Monte-Carlo linearity analysis (report your numbers).

Likely interview questions:

  • What limits the speed of a SAR ADC?
  • How does capacitor mismatch show up in DNL?
  • Why is comparator kickback a problem?
  • What does the Walden FoM capture and what does it miss?

Risks and fallback

Mixed-signal simulation in ngspice is slow and fiddly. Fallback: transistor-level DAC and comparator with behavioural SAR logic.

Extensions

  • Add a split-capacitor DAC and compare area and linearity
  • Drive it with the biquad anti-alias filter of MVE314TA-P3

Engineering problem

In human-robot collaboration a cobot must slow down or stop when a person comes too close (speed and separation monitoring in ISO/TS 15066). The interlock logic must be provably correct, and formal methods give strong evidence.

Proposed solution and architecture

Design a dual-channel interlock FSM that takes two independent distance sensors, computes the protective separation distance for the current robot speed, and commands normal, reduced-speed or protective stop, with voting, a watchdog and safe defaults when the sensors disagree. Prove safety properties with SymbiYosys and test with injected sensor faults.

Dual-channel distance inputs→Separation-distance check→Voting→Watchdog→Mode FSM→Formal properties
FPGA note: board optional. RTL simulation plus synthesis reports is a valid path; results are then simulation and synthesis estimates, not on-board measurements.

Implementation plan

  1. Study: ISO/TS 15066 speed and separation monitoring, protective separation distance. Gate: hazard list and the distance formula with your parameters.
  2. Design: fixed-point separation check and the mode FSM in Verilog. Gate: simulation of approach scenarios.
  3. Write properties (never normal speed inside the protective distance; sensor disagreement forces a safe state; watchdog timeout stops the robot). Gate: properties reviewed by a peer.
  4. Prove with SymbiYosys (bounded and, where possible, unbounded with k-induction). Gate: proofs pass or counterexamples fixed.
  5. Inject sensor faults (stuck, delayed, noisy) in simulation; measure reaction time and report diagnostic coverage.
